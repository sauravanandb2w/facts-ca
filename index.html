How the climate architecture fits together

The UNFCCC is the parent convention. It does not itself freeze emission numbers for every country; it creates the Conference of the Parties (COP), subsidiary bodies, reporting routines, and the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities (CBDR-RC). Two major treaties were later adopted under this roof: the Kyoto Protocol (top-down quantified targets mainly for developed Annex-I Parties) and the Paris Agreement (bottom-up Nationally Determined Contributions for all Parties, with a collective temperature aim).

Meetings often run in parallel: COP (UNFCCC), CMP (Kyoto), CMA (Paris). Exam questions love mixing these three acronyms.

COP–CMP–CMA: COP = Convention, CMP = Kyoto Protocol, CMA = Paris Agreement — “ए” = Agreement।

COP-by-COP: what actually changed

  • COP3 Kyoto (1997): First period quantified emission limitation/reduction objectives (QELROs) for Annex I; created CDM, Joint Implementation, and Emissions Trading. Developing countries (including India) had no binding QELROs.
  • COP15 Copenhagen (2009): Political Copenhagen Accords — 2°C recognition, pledge-and-review flavour, $100 bn/year climate finance goal language. Not a full legal successor to Kyoto, but shaped later bottom-up thinking.
  • COP21 Paris (2015): Paris Agreement — NDCs; well below 2°C and pursue 1.5°C; 5-year cycles; Enhanced Transparency Framework; Global Stocktake; Article 6 cooperative approaches.
  • COP26 Glasgow (2021): Glasgow Climate Pact — first COP cover decision language on phasing down unabated coal and inefficient fossil fuel subsidies; completed much of the Paris rulebook (including Article 6); India’s Panchamrit / net-zero 2070 announcement belongs to this diplomatic moment.
  • COP27 Sharm el-Sheikh (2022): Historic agreement to establish a Loss and Damage Fund for vulnerable countries — a long-standing demand of the Global South.
  • COP28 Dubai (2023): First Global Stocktake outcome; language on transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems; operational progress on Loss & Damage Fund arrangements.
  • COP29 Baku (2024): Finance-centred COP — New Collective Quantified Goal (NCQG) on climate finance dominated headlines.
  • COP30 Belém, Brazil (2025): Amazon / forests / nature–climate interface expected to feature heavily in CA; treat as a living current-affairs file.

Paris Agreement — provisions you must unpack

Mitigation & temperature

Long-term temperature goal: hold increase to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursue efforts to limit to 1.5°C. Each Party prepares, communicates and maintains successive NDCs. Ambition is expected to progress over time (progression principle).

Transparency & stocktake

Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF) for action and support. Global Stocktake every five years assesses collective progress — first GST concluded at COP28. NDCs are then meant to be informed by that stocktake.

Article 6 markets

6.2 cooperative approaches / ITMOs (internationally transferred mitigation outcomes); 6.4 Paris crediting mechanism (successor logic to CDM); 6.8 non-market approaches. Double-counting and corresponding adjustments are the exam traps.

Climate finance & funds — do not collapse into one name

  • Green Climate Fund (GCF): operating entity of the UNFCCC financial mechanism; HQ Songdo, Republic of Korea; serves Paris as well.
  • Global Environment Facility (GEF): older financial mechanism serving multiple conventions (including UNFCCC, CBD, UNCCD, chemicals conventions in various roles).
  • Adaptation Fund: born under Kyoto (initially financed partly by a share of CDM proceeds); continues to serve adaptation under the Paris era.
  • Loss & Damage Fund: decided at COP27, operational steps thereafter — distinct from mitigation/adaptation finance narratives in many questions.
  • Special Climate Change Fund / Least Developed Countries Fund: often appear in older Prelims materials; know they exist under the Convention’s finance architecture.

India’s climate pledges (keep numbers exact)

  • Updated NDC (to UNFCCC): reduce emissions intensity of GDP by 45% by 2030 from 2005 levels; about 50% cumulative electric power installed capacity from non-fossil fuel-based energy resources by 2030; create an additional carbon sink of 2.5 to 3 billion tonnes of CO₂ equivalent through additional forest and tree cover by 2030.
  • Net-zero by 2070 — announced by the PM at COP26 (political long-term goal; distinct from the 2030 NDC package).
  • Panchamrit (COP26 framing): non-fossil capacity, renewables, intensity, carbon sink, and net-zero 2070 — revise from a trusted brief before the exam because wording in coaching material sometimes drifts.
  • Domestic policy bridges: NAPCC + State Action Plans; National Solar Mission lineage; Perform Achieve Trade (PAT); Green Hydrogen Mission; LiFE; International Solar Alliance (ISA is diplomacy/energy, not a UNFCCC protocol).

भारत NDC: “पैंतालीस–पचास–ढाई/तीन–सत्तर” — 45% intensity ↓, 50% non-fossil, 2.5–3 Gt sink, net-zero 2070।

1. UNFCCC — United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change
Adopted 1992 · Rio Earth Summit In force 21 Mar 1994 Secretariat: Bonn India: Party

The UNFCCC is the foundational multilateral environmental agreement on climate change. Its ultimate objective (Article 2) is to achieve stabilization of greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere at a level that would prevent dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system — within a time-frame that allows ecosystems to adapt naturally, ensures food production is not threatened, and enables sustainable economic development.

Core principles & structure
  • CBDR-RC: common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities — the philosophical spine of North–South climate politics.
  • Annex I: industrialised countries + economies in transition — historically expected to take the lead on mitigation.
  • Annex II: a subset of Annex I with additional obligations to provide financial resources and facilitate technology transfer to developing countries.
  • Non-Annex I: mostly developing countries, including India.
  • Institutional engine: COP (supreme body), SBI (Implementation), SBSTA (Scientific and Technological Advice), Bureau, and the secretariat in Bonn.
What Parties actually do under the Convention

Publish inventories and national communications / biennial reports (formats evolved over time), formulate programmes containing measures to mitigate climate change and facilitate adaptation, promote science and education, and cooperate on finance and technology. The Convention itself did not lock every country into Kyoto-style numerical caps; that came later via the Protocol and then Paris.

India angle

India has consistently argued equity, per-capita emissions, historical responsibility, and the right to development. Domestic planning bridges include the National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC) with its missions, State Action Plans, and the evolving Long-Term Low Emission Development Strategy submitted in the Paris era.

Trap: UNFCCC ≠ Kyoto ≠ Paris. Parent / Protocol / Agreement. COP / CMP / CMA.
2. Kyoto Protocol — design, mechanisms, commitment periods
Adopted 1997 · COP3 Kyoto In force 16 Feb 2005 Governing body: CMP India: Party, no Annex-I QELRO

Kyoto operationalised the Convention for a first generation of binding targets. Annex I Parties accepted quantified emission limitation and reduction objectives for listed greenhouse gases over commitment periods. The first commitment period ran 2008–2012. The Doha Amendment created a second commitment period (2013–2020); its politics and ratification story are less central now than Paris, but the architecture still appears in Prelims comparisons.

Flexible mechanisms (still asked)
  • Clean Development Mechanism (CDM): Annex I Party earns certified emission reductions (CERs) from projects in non-Annex I countries — sustainable development + cost-effective compliance narrative. India was a major CDM host.
  • Joint Implementation (JI): among Annex I Parties (often involving economies in transition).
  • International Emissions Trading: trading of assigned amount units among Annex I Parties.

क्योटो लचीले तंत्र — “सीधा जा ईटी”: CDM, JI, Emissions Trading।

Why Paris replaced Kyoto’s political model

Kyoto’s hard split left large emerging emitters without QELROs and some major emitters outside the regime at key moments. Paris shifted to universal participation with nationally determined pledges, while keeping differentiation language softer and more flexible.

3. Paris Agreement — articles, cycles, India NDCs
Adopted 12 Dec 2015 · COP21 In force 4 Nov 2016 Governing body: CMA India ratified Oct 2016

Paris is a legally binding agreement (as a treaty instrument) that combines binding process obligations with nationally determined substance. It aims to strengthen the global response by holding temperature increase to well below 2°C and pursuing 1.5°C, increasing adaptation ability, and making finance flows consistent with low-emission climate-resilient development.

Machinery you should be able to explain in 4–5 lines
  • NDCs: each Party’s self-defined contribution; communicated every five years; expected to represent a progression.
  • Global Stocktake: collective progress check every five years; first GST at COP28 informs the next round of NDCs.
  • Enhanced Transparency Framework: reporting and review so pledges can be tracked without Kyoto’s Annex-I-only compliance psychology.
  • Article 6: voluntary cooperation — market and non-market — with rules against double counting.
  • Adaptation & loss and damage: Paris recognises the importance of averting, minimising and addressing loss and damage (Article 8); the dedicated Fund was a later COP political breakthrough.
India’s updated NDC package (memorise)
  • Emissions intensity of GDP: −45% by 2030 vs 2005.
  • ~50% cumulative installed electric power capacity from non-fossil resources by 2030.
  • Additional carbon sink of 2.5–3 GtCO₂e through additional forest & tree cover by 2030.
  • Long-term political goal: net-zero by 2070 (COP26).

पेरिस लय: “पाँच साल NDC, पाँच साल Stocktake” — पहला GST = COP28 दुबई।

4. IPCC — science body, not a treaty
Established 1988 Parents: WMO + UNEP HQ: Geneva Products: Assessment Reports, Special Reports, Methodology Reports

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change assesses the science related to climate change. It does not conduct new primary research in the way a lab does; it assesses and synthesises peer-reviewed literature. It does not prescribe policy, though policymakers use its reports intensively.

Working Groups
  • WG I: physical science basis.
  • WG II: impacts, adaptation, vulnerability.
  • WG III: mitigation of climate change.
  • TFI (Task Force on National Greenhouse Gas Inventories): inventory methodologies used by countries.

IPCC ग्रुप्स — “विज्ञान–असर–रोकथाम”: WG1 science, WG2 impacts/adaptation, WG3 mitigation। याद रखो — IPCC संधि नहीं।

Landmark products often in CA: AR5, AR6, Special Report on 1.5°C (SR1.5), SRCCL (land), SROCC (ocean & cryosphere). Exam trap: confusing IPCC with UNFCCC secretariat or with a funding body.

5. CBD — Convention on Biological Diversity
Adopted 1992 Rio In force 29 Dec 1993 Secretariat: Montreal India: Party · Biological Diversity Act 2002

The CBD is the main global treaty for biodiversity. It has three equally important objectives: (1) conservation of biological diversity; (2) sustainable use of its components; and (3) fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising out of the utilisation of genetic resources. Sovereignty of States over their natural resources is recognised, alongside a duty not to cause damage to the environment of other States or areas beyond national jurisdiction.

CBD के तीन लक्ष्य — “बचाओ–बरतो–बाँटो”: Conservation, Sustainable use, Benefit sharing (ABS)।

Institutions & strategies
  • COP is the governing body; SBSTTA provides scientific advice.
  • National Biodiversity Strategies and Action Plans (NBSAPs) are the principal domestic planning instruments.
  • Aichi Biodiversity Targets (2011–2020) were the previous decade’s framework; most were not fully achieved globally.
  • Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF) adopted at CBD COP15 (2022): includes the headline “30×30” style targets (protect at least 30% of terrestrial and inland water areas and of marine/coastal areas by 2030 — verify exact target text when revising), plus targets on restoration, pollution, subsidies, ABS, and finance.
India domestic bridge

Biological Diversity Act, 2002; National Biodiversity Authority (Chennai); State Biodiversity Boards; Biodiversity Management Committees; people’s biodiversity registers. ABS Regulations operationalise Nagoya-aligned access rules. Questions often ask which body grants approval for access to biological resources / associated knowledge.

6. Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety
Adopted 2000 In force 2003 Protocol to CBD Tool: Advance Informed Agreement (AIA)

Cartagena addresses the safe transfer, handling and use of Living Modified Organisms (LMOs) resulting from modern biotechnology that may have adverse effects on biodiversity, also taking risks to human health into account. It is especially about transboundary movement.

Key operational ideas
  • Advance Informed Agreement (AIA): procedure ensuring the importing Party is informed and consents before the first intentional transboundary movement of an LMO for intentional introduction into the environment.
  • Biosafety Clearing-House (BCH): information exchange mechanism.
  • Risk assessment / risk management obligations; documentation for shipments.
  • Nagoya–Kuala Lumpur Supplementary Protocol (on Liability and Redress) is a related later instrument — know the name even if details are secondary.
Trap: Cartagena = biosafety of LMOs. Nagoya = ABS of genetic resources. Do not swap.

कार्टाजेना = “कार्गो LMO सुरक्षित लाओ” (Biosafety / AIA)। नागोया = “नफा बाँटो” (Access + Benefit Sharing)।

7. Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit Sharing
Adopted 2010 · Nagoya, Japan In force 12 Oct 2014 Protocol to CBD India: Party · BD Act / ABS Guidelines

Nagoya implements the CBD’s third objective. It sets rules for access to genetic resources and for the fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from their utilisation, including from associated traditional knowledge where applicable.

Core legal tools
  • Prior Informed Consent (PIC) of the provider country (and relevant indigenous/local communities where domestic law so requires).
  • Mutually Agreed Terms (MAT) — contract-like terms on benefit sharing (monetary and non-monetary).
  • Compliance measures in user countries; checkpoints; internationally recognised certificates of compliance via the ABS Clearing-House.
  • Covers utilisation of genetic resources (R&D on genetic/biochemical composition) — biotechnology and pharma narratives dominate examples.

नागोया चाबी — “पहले इजाज़त (PIC), फिर शर्तें (MAT), फिर लाभ बाँटो”। भारत में NBA / BD Act 2002 से जोड़ो।

India’s Biological Diversity Act regime (National Biodiversity Authority approvals, benefit-sharing arrangements) is the classic domestic linkage in UPSC questions.

8. CITES — trade in endangered species
Adopted 1973 Washington In force 1975 Secretariat: Geneva India Party since 1976

CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora) regulates international trade in specimens of wild animals and plants to ensure trade does not threaten survival. It is not a habitat-protection convention like CBD/Ramsar; its lever is permits and listings.

Appendices — learn the logic, not only the labels
  • Appendix I: species threatened with extinction. Commercial international trade is generally prohibited; limited non-commercial trade may be allowed with strict permits from both exporting and importing countries.
  • Appendix II: species not necessarily threatened with extinction now, but trade must be controlled to avoid utilisation incompatible with survival. Trade allowed with export permits (and other checks) if not detrimental to survival.
  • Appendix III: species protected in at least one country that has asked others for assistance in controlling trade. Listing can be done by a Party for its own species with a different procedure from App I/II CoP votes.

CITES अपेंडिक्स — “एक बंद, दो कंट्रोल, तीन मदद माँगो”: I = व्यापार लगभग बंद, II = नियंत्रित व्यापार, III = एक देश ने मदद माँगी।

Institutions & India bridge

Conference of the Parties amends Appendices; Animals Committee and Plants Committee provide technical advice; Standing Committee oversees between CoPs. In India, CITES implementation intertwines with the Wildlife (Protection) Act, customs controls, and designated Management/Scientific Authorities. Recent CoPs often put sharks, rays, timber trees, and exotic pets into the news — perfect CA fodder.

9. CMS — Convention on Migratory Species (Bonn Convention)
Adopted 1979 Bonn In force 1983 Secretariat: Bonn India: Party

CMS conserves migratory species that periodically cross one or more national jurisdictional boundaries. “Range States” are countries that exercise jurisdiction over any part of the range of a migratory species.

Appendices & daughter instruments
  • Appendix I: endangered migratory species — Parties prohibit taking except in tightly limited circumstances.
  • Appendix II: species that have an unfavourable conservation status and need or would significantly benefit from international cooperation — basis for Agreements and MoUs.
  • CMS family includes legally binding Agreements and lighter MoUs (e.g. on raptors, dugong, sharks, Siberian crane — India-linked examples appear frequently in coaching lists; confirm the latest MoU set before quoting).

CMS = “घुमंतू प्रजाति बॉन में” — Appendix I = खतरे में (taking बंद), II = सहयोग/MoU चाहिए।

India has also used CMS COP hosting/diplomacy in recent years as a current-affairs hook — pair the instrument with whatever latest COP host/outcome is in the news when you revise.

10. IUCN Red List — categories (instrument of science, not a treaty)
IUCN (NGO + government members) HQ association: Gland, Switzerland Output: Red List of Threatened Species

The IUCN Red List is the global standard for assessing extinction risk. Categories from most to least severe among threatened/extinct classes commonly tested: Extinct (EX), Extinct in the Wild (EW), Critically Endangered (CR), Endangered (EN), Vulnerable (VU), then Near Threatened (NT), Least Concern (LC), plus Data Deficient (DD) and Not Evaluated (NE).

CR + EN + VU together are the “threatened” categories. A Red List assessment informs CITES debates, national wildlife schedules, and protected-area priorities, but an IUCN listing by itself is not an international trade ban — that is CITES / domestic law.

IUCN रेड लिस्ट सीढ़ी — “बिलकुल गया → जंगल में गया → बहुत खतरा → खतरा → कमज़ोर”: EX → EW → CR → EN → VU। Threatened = CR+EN+VU।

11. Ramsar Convention — wetlands in depth
Adopted 2 Feb 1971 · Ramsar, Iran In force 1975 Secretariat: Gland, Switzerland India Party: 1 Feb 1982

Ramsar is the oldest of the modern global intergovernmental MEAs for a specific ecosystem type. Its mission is conservation and wise use of all wetlands through local and national actions and international cooperation, as a contribution to sustainable development. “Wetlands” under Ramsar are defined broadly — marshes, lakes, rivers, peatlands, mangroves, coral reefs, and even human-made sites like tanks and reservoirs can qualify.

Three pillars of implementation
  1. Designation of suitable wetlands to the List of Wetlands of International Importance (Ramsar List) and ensuring their effective management.
  2. Wise use of all wetlands in the Party’s territory (not only listed ones) — maintaining ecological character within sustainable development.
  3. International cooperation on shared wetlands, river basins, and migratory species pathways.

रामसर तीन खम्भे — “लिस्ट करो, समझदारी से बरतो (wise use), मिलकर बचाओ”। मोंट्रू रिकॉर्ड = “मुसीबत वाली रामसर साइटें”। विश्व वेटलैंड दिवस = 2 फ़रवरी (रामसर जन्मतिथि)।

Listing criteria & Montreux Record

There are nine criteria grouping sites of international importance (representative/unique wetlands; species/ecological communities; waterbirds; fish; other taxa). Once listed, the ecological character should be maintained. The Montreux Record is a register of listed sites where changes in ecological character have occurred, are occurring, or are likely to occur because of human interference — a danger/watch list inside Ramsar, not a separate convention.

Institutions

COP (every 3 years typically), Standing Committee, Scientific & Technical Review Panel (STRP), and the Secretariat hosted by IUCN in Gland. World Wetlands Day is celebrated on 2 February.

India — law, sites, exam caution
  • India’s Ramsar count has expanded rapidly in recent years; always verify the latest official count from MoEFCC / Ramsar before quoting a number in Mains.
  • Classic early sites discussed in older notes include Chilika Lake (Odisha) and Keoladeo (Bharatpur). Chilika is also famous for Irrawaddy dolphins and lagoon ecology; Keoladeo for migratory waterfowl.
  • Indian sites frequently associated with the Montreux Record in standard materials: Keoladeo National Park and Loktak Lake — re-check the live Montreux list before the exam.
  • Domestic framework: Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2017; National Plan for Conservation of Aquatic Ecosystems (NPCA); state wetland authorities.
12. UNCCD — desertification, land degradation & drought
Adopted 1994 Paris In force 1996 Secretariat: Bonn Rio Convention #3 with UNFCCC & CBD

The United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification addresses desertification and mitigates the effects of drought, particularly in Africa, but with global membership. It focuses on arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas. Implementation emphasises bottom-up participatory approaches, National Action Programmes, and synergy with climate and biodiversity agendas.

Land Degradation Neutrality (LDN)

LDN is a state whereby the amount and quality of land resources necessary to support ecosystem functions and services remains stable or increases. It is reflected in SDG Target 15.3. Parties set voluntary LDN targets. India has announced LDN targets and links restoration programmes (afforestation, watershed, agroforestry) to this frame.

UNCCD = “धूल–सूखा–ज़मीन” कन्वेंशन। LDN = भूमि क्षरण तटस्थता — जितना बिगड़े उतना सुधारो (net zero land degradation)। SDG 15.3 से जोड़ो।

Bodies

COP; Committee on Science and Technology (CST); Committee for the Review of the Implementation of the Convention (CRIC). The Great Green Wall (Africa) and similar landscape initiatives often appear as CA examples of UNCCD-aligned action.

13. Vienna Convention, Montreal Protocol & Kigali Amendment
Vienna 1985 (framework) Montreal 1987 (controls) Kigali Amendment 2016 (HFCs) Ozone Secretariat: Nairobi

The Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer created the cooperative framework. The Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer is the control treaty — widely called the most successful MEA because it established binding, time-bound phase-out schedules for ozone-depleting substances (CFCs, halons, methyl bromide, HCFCs, etc.), supported by the Multilateral Fund for developing-country compliance.

Important distinctions
  • ODS vs GHG: classic ODS destroy stratospheric ozone. Some replacements (HFCs) do not deplete ozone much but are potent greenhouse gases — which is why Kigali matters for climate.
  • Article 5 Parties: developing countries with delayed control schedules and financial support eligibility.
  • Kigali Amendment (2016): phasedown of HFCs under Montreal. Different country baselines and freeze/reduction calendars apply; India is an Article 5 Party with a later schedule than non-A5 Parties.
India bridge

India Cooling Action Plan (ICAP) — aims to reduce cooling demand, refrigerant transition, and energy efficiency together. Domestic ODS rules under environment/ozone cell administration are the bureaucratic side rarely asked in depth, but ICAP + Kigali is high-yield.

ओज़ोन कहानी — “वियना ने छतरी दी, मॉन्ट्रियल ने ODS काटी, किगाली ने HFC घटाई”। ODS ≠ HFC; HFC ओज़ोन नहीं खाते, जलवायु गरम करते हैं।

14. Basel Convention — hazardous wastes & plastics
Adopted 1989 In force 1992 Secretariat: Geneva PIC + ESM logic

Basel controls the transboundary movements of hazardous wastes and other wastes and obliges Parties to ensure environmentally sound management (ESM). It was a response to dumping of hazardous wastes in developing countries. Exports generally require Prior Informed Consent of the importing (and often transit) State.

Amendments UPSC cares about
  • Ban Amendment: prohibits certain hazardous waste exports from developed (Annex VII) to developing countries — political equity story.
  • Plastic waste amendments (2019): brought many plastic waste streams into a clearer PIC framework — directly relevant to global waste trade and India’s import restrictions / PWM Rules debates.

Basel is about wastes in movement and management, not a chemical ban list like Stockholm.

बेसल = “कचरा सीमा पार तभी जब इजाज़त हो” (PIC + ESM)। प्लास्टिक वेस्ट 2019 संशोधन याद रखो।

15. Rotterdam Convention — Prior Informed Consent for chemicals
Adopted 1998 In force 2004 FAO + UNEP Annex III listed chemicals

Rotterdam creates a legally binding Prior Informed Consent (PIC) procedure for certain hazardous chemicals and pesticides in international trade. When a chemical is listed in Annex III, exporting Parties must ensure that exports do not proceed contrary to the importing Party’s documented import decision.

Critical trap: Rotterdam does not automatically ban a chemical worldwide. It empowers informed decisions. Listing fights at COP (e.g. over particular pesticides) are recurring CA items.

रॉटरडैम = “पहले पूछो, फिर भेजो” (Prior Informed Consent)। लिस्टिंग ≠ दुनियाभर में बैन।

16. Stockholm Convention — Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs)
Adopted 2001 In force 2004 Secretariat: Geneva POPs: persistent, bioaccumulative, toxic, long-range

Stockholm targets Persistent Organic Pollutants — chemicals that remain intact for long periods, become widely distributed, accumulate in fatty tissue, and are toxic to humans and wildlife. The Convention requires Parties to eliminate or restrict production/use and to reduce unintentional releases.

Annex logic
  • Annex A: elimination (with specific exemptions possible).
  • Annex B: restriction (e.g. DDT historically framed for acceptable purpose of disease vector control under conditions).
  • Annex C: unintentionally produced POPs (e.g. dioxins/furans) — best available techniques / best environmental practices.

New chemicals can be added by COP after scientific review (POPs Review Committee). India’s National Implementation Plan and domestic regulations on POPs appear as applied questions.

स्टॉकहोम POPs — Annex याद: “A हटाओ, B बाधित करो, C अनजाने उत्सर्जन घटाओ” (Eliminate / Restrict / Unintentional)।

17. Minamata Convention — mercury
Adopted 2013 · Kumamoto, Japan In force 2017 Named after Minamata disease tragedy India: Party

Minamata addresses the full life cycle of mercury: supply (mines), trade, mercury-added products (certain batteries, lamps, cosmetics, etc. on schedules), manufacturing processes, artisanal and small-scale gold mining (ASGM), air emissions, releases to land/water, environmentally sound interim storage, mercury wastes, and contaminated sites.

It is the chemicals convention most tightly tied to a public-health disaster narrative (methylmercury poisoning in Minamata, Japan). Distinguish it from Stockholm (many POPs) and Basel (waste movements).

मिनामाता = पारा (mercury) की पूरी ज़िंदगी कंट्रोल — खान से कचरे तक। नाम जापान की बीमारी से।

18. BRS cluster — how Basel, Rotterdam & Stockholm work together

The three conventions share a chemicals-and-waste family resemblance and often hold coordinated COPs (BRS COPs) to exploit administrative synergies. Still, their legal triggers differ:

19. UNCLOS — zones, rights, institutions (full ladder)
Adopted 1982 Montego Bay In force 16 Nov 1994 India ratified 1995 ITLOS · ISA · CLCS · DOALOS

The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea is often called the “constitution of the oceans.” It codifies maritime zones measured mainly from baselines, balances coastal-state rights with freedoms of navigation, and creates institutions for the deep seabed and dispute settlement.

Zone ladder (memorise breadth + legal character)
ZoneBreadthLegal character / rights
Internal watersLandward of baselineFull sovereignty (like land territory)
Territorial SeaUp to 12 nmSovereignty; foreign ships enjoy innocent passage
Contiguous ZoneUp to 24 nmEnforcement for customs, fiscal, immigration, sanitary laws
EEZUp to 200 nmSovereign rights for exploring, exploiting, conserving, managing natural resources (living & non-living); jurisdiction over artificial islands, marine scientific research, environmental protection — not full sovereignty
Continental ShelfAt least 200 nm; may extend to 350 nm under Art. 76 formulaSovereign rights over seabed/subsoil mineral & sedentary species resources
High seasBeyond EEZFreedoms of navigation, overflight, cables/pipelines, fishing, scientific research, etc., with duties (e.g. conservation, due regard)
The AreaSeabed/ocean floor beyond national jurisdictionCommon Heritage of Mankind; mineral resources managed via International Seabed Authority (ISA, Kingston)
Institutions
  • ITLOS (Hamburg): International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea.
  • ISA (Kingston): organises and controls activities in the Area, especially deep-sea mining regime (exploration regulations exist; exploitation regulations have been politically contested — CA hotspot).
  • CLCS: Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf — makes recommendations on outer CS limits beyond 200 nm.
  • DOALOS: UN Division for Ocean Affairs and the Law of the Sea — secretariat support functions.
Classic trap: Contiguous Zone ≠ EEZ. CZ is limited enforcement jurisdiction; EEZ is resource sovereign rights out to 200 nm. Another trap: “Common Heritage” applies to the Area’s resources, not to the Territorial Sea.

UNCLOS दूरियाँ — “बारह राज, चौबीस जाँच, दो सौ दौलत”: 12 nm Territorial Sea (संप्रभुता), 24 nm Contiguous (customs/fiscal/immigration/sanitary), 200 nm EEZ (संसाधन अधिकार)। Area = “मानव जाति की साझी विरासत” (Common Heritage) — ISA Kingston।

20. BBNJ Agreement — High Seas Treaty (2023)
Adopted 19 June 2023 Implementing agreement under UNCLOS Focus: Areas Beyond National Jurisdiction (ABNJ) India: track signature / ratification status before exam

Formally the Agreement under UNCLOS on the Conservation and Sustainable Use of Marine Biological Diversity of Areas Beyond National Jurisdiction. It fills governance gaps on the high seas and in the Area regarding biodiversity — places where UNCLOS set the stage but lacked a modern package on MPAs, benefit sharing from marine genetic resources, and standardised EIAs.

Four pillars
  1. Marine Genetic Resources (MGRs) including digital sequence information debates, and fair & equitable benefit sharing.
  2. Area-based management tools, including Marine Protected Areas on the high seas.
  3. Environmental Impact Assessments for activities with potential effects in ABNJ.
  4. Capacity-building and transfer of marine technology, especially for developing countries.

Entry into force depends on reaching the required number of ratifications — treat the in-force status as a living CA fact. Links tightly to CBD’s 30×30 target because much of the ocean lies beyond EEZs.

BBNJ चार खम्भे — “जेनेटिक बाँटो, MPA बनाओ, EIA करो, क्षमता दो”: MGRs + benefit sharing, area-based tools/MPAs, EIAs, capacity-building।

Ramsar × India — applied revision

  • Wise use ≠ no use: ecological character can be maintained alongside sustainable livelihoods (fisheries, regulated tourism, traditional use).
  • Listed vs all wetlands: listing creates international importance status; wise-use duties cover wetlands generally.
  • Montreux Record: ecological character under threat/change — Keoladeo & Loktak are the usual Indian examples in standard notes; verify live list.
  • Legal hinge: Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2017 define wetlands, restrict harmful activities, and push state-level wetland authorities; NPCA is the central scheme umbrella for aquatic ecosystems.
  • CA habit: when a new Indian Ramsar site is added, note State, ecosystem type (mangrove / high-altitude lake / river floodplain), and any unique species — that pattern repeats every year.

UNCLOS — worked examples of exam traps

  • A research ship in your EEZ is not in your internal waters; coastal state has jurisdiction over marine scientific research in the EEZ, but freedoms of navigation still matter.
  • Sedentary species on the continental shelf fall under shelf sovereign rights even if the water column is high seas beyond the EEZ (advanced but asked).
  • Deep-sea mining in the Area ≠ mining on your continental shelf; ISA regime vs coastal-state shelf rights.
  • Innocent passage applies in the territorial sea; transit passage is the different regime used for international straits (know the distinction exists).

Practice MCQs — Environment conventions

Attempt first, then reveal. Answers stay hidden until you click.

1. Which of the following is not one of the three Rio Conventions of 1992?
Answer: b. Rio trio = UNFCCC, CBD, UNCCD. Montreal Protocol (1987) is the ozone treaty under the Vienna Convention.
2. The secretariat of the Ramsar Convention is located in:
Answer: c. Ramsar Secretariat is in Gland (IUCN campus). Bonn hosts UNFCCC/UNCCD/CMS; Montreal hosts CBD; Nairobi hosts Ozone Secretariat.
3. With reference to the Paris Agreement, consider the following:
1. It follows a bottom-up approach of Nationally Determined Contributions.
2. The first Global Stocktake was completed at COP28 (Dubai).
3. Only Annex-I Parties are required to submit NDCs.
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
Answer: a. All Parties submit NDCs — not only Annex I. Statement 3 is wrong.
4. The Nagoya Protocol is related to:
Answer: b. Cartagena = biosafety (LMOs). Nagoya = ABS. Kigali = HFCs. Basel = hazardous wastes.
5. Under UNCLOS, the Contiguous Zone extends up to:
Answer: b. TS = 12 nm; CZ = 24 nm; EEZ = 200 nm; CS may extend toward 350 nm under Article 76 formula.
6. Which protocol specifically addresses Prior Informed Consent for certain hazardous chemicals and pesticides in international trade?
Answer: b. Rotterdam = PIC for chemicals/pesticides. Cartagena uses Advance Informed Agreement for LMOs. Stockholm = POPs. Minamata = mercury.
7. The Kigali Amendment is to the:
Answer: c. Kigali Amendment (2016) to the Montreal Protocol phases down HFCs.
8. “Common Heritage of Mankind” under UNCLOS primarily applies to:
Answer: c. Resources of the Area are the common heritage of mankind, administered via the International Seabed Authority.
9. Appendix I of CITES generally means:
Answer: b. App I = endangered, commercial trade banned (with limited exceptions). App II = controlled trade. App III = unilateral listing needing cooperation.
10. The BBNJ Agreement is best described as:
Answer: b. High Seas Treaty / BBNJ — conservation & sustainable use of marine biodiversity in areas beyond national jurisdiction, under UNCLOS.
11. Which fund is specifically associated with the Kyoto track and also serves Paris-era adaptation finance?
Answer: a. Adaptation Fund originated under Kyoto (share of CDM proceeds) and continues under the Paris era. GEF is broader; NDB/CRA are BRICS finance, not UNFCCC adaptation funds.
12. Montreux Record is associated with:
Answer: b. Montreux Record tracks Ramsar sites where ecological character has changed, is changing, or is likely to change.
